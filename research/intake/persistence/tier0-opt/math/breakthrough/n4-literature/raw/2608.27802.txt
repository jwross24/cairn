                                             THE MULTIPLICATIVE PERSISTENCE CONJECTURE: RESOLVING THE
                                                  2-ADIC OBSTRUCTION FOR NONZERO EVEN TARGETS

                                                                                          PATRICK NYADJO FONGA


                                                  Abstract. The multiplicative persistence problem studies the process of repeatedly replacing a
                                                  positive integer by the product of its digits until a single digit, called the terminal digit, is reached.
                                                  The classical conjecture asserts that no decimal integer requires more than 11 iterations. Brier,




arXiv:2608.27802v1 [math.NT] 28 Aug 2026
                                                  Clavier, Gutsche, and Naccache proved the conjecture for all odd terminal digits. In their approach
                                                  to nonzero even terminal digits, they were led to infinite families of decimal integers in which
                                                  the numbers of digits 2, . . . , 9 are fixed, while arbitrarily many digits 1 may be inserted. They
                                                  conjectured that, despite the infinitude of such a family, the exponent of 2 dividing its elements
                                                  is uniformly bounded. We prove this conjecture and obtain an explicit bound depending only on
                                                  the prescribed digit multiplicities. More generally, our argument applies in every base b ≥ 3 and to
                                                  every prime p | b. In base 10, combining our bound with the method of Brier, Clavier, Gutsche, and
                                                  Naccache yields a finite algorithm for a further analysis of each nonzero even terminal digit.

Perez and Styer performed interesting computations in bases 2 through 12 and in the particular

A useful way to study the problem is to work backward from the terminal digit. Brier, Clavier,
Gutsche, and Naccache [2] developed this approach in base 10. They showed that Conjecture A holds
for odd terminal digits: indeed, they proved that terminal digits 1, 3, 7, and 9 lead to persistence at
most 1, while terminal digit 5 leads to persistence at most 5. However, their approach to the even
terminal digits 2, 4, 6, and 8 leads to exponential Diophantine equations whose analysis gives rise to
the following conjecture [2, Conjecture 2];

Conjecture B. Fix nonnegative integers ν2 , . . . , ν9 . Consider the family of positive decimal integers
containing exactly νd occurrences of the digit d, for each d = 2, . . . , 9, an arbitrary number of
occurrences of the digit 1, and no occurrence of the digit 0. Then there exists a constant C such
that v2 (n) ≤ C for every integer n in this family.

In this paper, we provide a proof for Conjecture B; see Corollary 3.2. In fact, we obtain a more
general result that applies to arbitrary bases; see Theorem 3.1
The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we establish an explicit uniform bound in every
base b ≥ 3 for every prime p | b; see Theorem 3.1. Since the bound found in Theorem 3.1 is not
optimal, Section 4 develops a finite recursive procedure, given in Theorem 4.1, for computing the
exact maximal valuation associated with a prescribed multiset of digits. Finally, Section 5 explains
how these results apply to the even-terminal-digit approach to multiplicative persistence.

section. Let b ≥ 3 and n ∈ N.
      • If the base-b expansion of n contains a zero digit, then Sb (n) = 0.
      • Inserting or removing digits equal to 1 in the base-b expansion of n does not change the
        value of Sb (n).
      • Reordering the digits in the base-b expansion of n does not change the value of Sb (n).

                    5. Consequences for nonzero even terminal digits

We now explain how our valuation bounds could contribute to the treatment of the nonzero even
terminal digits δ ∈ {2, 4, 6, 8}.
Throughout this section, S = S10 denotes the decimal digit-product map.
For a fixed terminal digit δ, let
                                         Aδ = (Vδ , Eδ )
denote its full backward graph. More precisely, Aδ is the smallest directed graph constructed as
follows. We begin with δ ∈ Vδ . Whenever s ∈ Vδ and x is a positive integer satisfying S 2 (x) = s,
we add the intermediate value y := S(x) to Vδ , together with the directed edge s −→ y. Thus, an
arrow s → y means that S(y) = s and that y itself occurs as the digit product of another integer.
The arrows point away from the terminal digit and toward its possible predecessors.
If Aδ was finite, then the length of its longest path would give a uniform bound on the persistence of
all such integers. Motivated by this observation, Brier, Clavier, Gutsche, and Naccache constructed
finite candidate graphs
                                              Bδ = (Uδ , Fδ )
for the nonzero even terminal digits [2, Appendix C]. Proving that Bδ = Aδ would show that these
graphs contain all possible predecessors and would therefore establish the corresponding persistence
bounds. Here Uδ is the proposed finite set of vertices and Fδ is the proposed set of directed edges
between them. By construction,
                                          Uδ ⊆ Vδ ,    Fδ ⊆ Eδ .
The remaining problem is to prove that no additional vertices are missing. Equivalently, one must
verify that
(17)                           s ∈ Uδ ,    S 2 (x) = s   =⇒    S(x) ∈ Uδ .
If (17) holds for every s ∈ Uδ , then Bδ = Aδ .

The purpose of the present work is to provide the missing theoretical bound and to reduce this

beyond the scope of this paper, the formulation developed here is intended to make that next step
accessible.
